UX DESIGN · INFORMATION ARCHITECTURE · TRAVEL

Rethinking how people find a place to stay
so every traveller can search by what matters to them.

OVERVIEW

StayFinder is a concept redesign of the search experience on short-stay booking platforms like Airbnb. I studied four very different travellers heading to New York State, mapped how they think and decide, and used that research to organize and design a stay finder that helps each of them find a place that feels right.

ROLELead UX Designer
TIMELINE6 weeks
TOOLSFigma, Canva
PROBLEM STATEMENT

Booking platforms organize stays around price, ratings and dates, but travellers choose for other reasons: a quiet place to work, a creative atmosphere, or a neighbourhood that fits the reason for their trip. How might we organize and present stays so different travellers can find the right one using the things that matter to them?

CONSTRAINTS

🧭 One platform, very different needs

Every traveller uses the same platform but wants something different: quiet and focus, creative energy, or well-organized and uncrowded places. One layout and one set of filters had to work for all of them.

📚 Designing from research, not testing

The design is grounded in persona research and established UX theory rather than testing with real users. That kept me honest about what the work shows: a well-reasoned design, ready to be tested next.

RESULT

A stay finder organized around how travellers decide

StayFinder starts with the reason for the trip. Travellers can browse stays, then narrow them with quick filters such as museums and cafés, parking, or near transit and books, and open a full set of location features, amenities and property types when they want more control.

Each listing shows what a place offers and what is nearby, so people can judge the atmosphere and surroundings, not just the price and rating.

01

Start from the reason for the trip

The home page speaks to different trip purposes, whether exploring museums, attending a book festival or needing a quiet place to work, then leads straight into available stays.

02

Filter by what matters

Quick filters and expandable groups for location features, amenities and property type let each traveller narrow the list by their own priorities, like being near transit, cafés or bookstores.

03

See the place and its surroundings

Each listing pairs photos and details with what the place offers and where it is, so travellers can picture the stay and its neighbourhood before booking.

THE PROCESS
RESEARCH INSIGHTS

Understanding how four travellers think and decide

I built four personas, Evan and Jannelle, a couple visiting Albany, Malik, a design student exploring graduate schools, and Evelyn, a writer attending a literary festival. For each, I looked at their cognitive load, mental models and decision making, using ideas from Norman, Sweller and others to understand what overwhelms them and what they look for.

KEY INSIGHT #1 Same platform, different needs. Jannelle needs quiet, Malik wants creative energy, and Evan prefers well-organized, uncrowded spaces.
KEY INSIGHT #2 Overload shapes choices. Busy, noisy or poorly organized options raise cognitive load, so travellers avoid them.
KEY INSIGHT #3 Feeling beats numbers. Malik and Evelyn weigh atmosphere and authenticity over price, ratings or popularity.
RESULT

Designing around how each traveller decides

The research showed me that one design does not fit everyone. Instead of asking how to make a better list of stays, I asked what each person needs to see to feel confident choosing one. That shaped what information the design needed to organize: quiet, atmosphere, location and what is nearby.

USER FLOW

Adding the traveller’s needs to the booking flow

I started by mapping the existing Airbnb booking flow, then modified it for each persona. In the new flows, travellers set their needs up front, view matched stays, and compare what is nearby, such as cafés, museums, transit or bookstores, before they decide to book.

INFORMATION ARCHITECTURE

Organizing information the way people think

To organize the content, I created a taxonomy for each persona, grouping their needs and interests into clear categories, and an ontology that maps how those things relate, such as what a traveller needs, prefers or avoids. This showed which details belong together and which matter most to each person.

USER JOURNEY

Finding where users struggle

Journey maps for each persona followed the trip from planning to attending, tracking their actions, emotions, goals, thoughts and pain points. For Evelyn, the pain points were listings that ignore cultural atmosphere and the difficulty of judging a neighbourhood’s vibe, which became opportunities to show walkability and atmosphere cues.

THE DESIGN
WIREFRAMES

Testing the structure in mid-fidelity

I began with mid-fidelity wireframes of the home page, the property list with filters, and a listing page, focusing on layout and hierarchy before any visual styling.

VISUAL DESIGN

A calm, focused look for browsing stays

For the high-fidelity design, I used a dark, calm interface that lets the photography lead. Clear headings, simple filters and generous spacing keep the page easy to scan, so the details that matter to each traveller stand out.

KEY TAKEAWAYS

🧠 One design does not work for everyone

Even on the same platform, each person had different needs. Organizing information around how users think, with a taxonomy, ontology and journey maps, made those differences visible.

🧪 Next, test with real users

This design comes from research and theory. If I continued, I would test it with real travellers and make it clearer and easier to use based on what I learned.
