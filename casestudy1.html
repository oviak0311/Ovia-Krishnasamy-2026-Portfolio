OVERVIEW

MedCheck is a medication checker for adults starting a new prescription or over-the-counter medicine, and the caregivers who help them. I designed and built it end to end, turning long, technical FDA drug labels into a clear, scannable page that answers one question: what should I watch for?

ROLE Lead UX Designer
TIMELINE 6 weeks
TOOLS Figma, HTML, CSS, JavaScript, openFDA API
PROBLEM STATEMENT

Official drug labels contain everything a person needs to know, but they’re long, dense and written for professionals. Someone starting a new medication, often anxious and short on time, has to dig through pages of text to find the side effects and warnings that matter. How might we help people quickly find what to watch for, without changing what the label says?

CONSTRAINTS

⏳ Working with messy label data

The FDA label data has no “common side effects” field. Each section is one long block of text, worded differently by every drug company, so early results were inconsistent: some medications showed no side effects, no drug class, or even the wrong product. The design had to work for every medication, not just the one on my screen.

⚕️ Accuracy over simplicity

Health information can’t be oversimplified. I could make the wording plainer, but not change the meaning, so every summary comes from the label’s own text, the full label is one click away, and the site is clear that it doesn’t replace advice from a doctor or pharmacist.

RESULT

A one-page medication checker that puts what to watch for first

Instead of searching through a full label, people type a medication name and see a short, structured page: common side effects first, then serious warnings, with any FDA boxed warning flagged at the top of that list. Longer details stay tucked into rows that open on demand.

The result feels calm and trustworthy, with every answer traceable back to the original label. I tested the finished site against 78 real medications to make sure the design held up beyond a single example. View the live site · View the source code

01

Search by brand or ingredient

People don’t always know the exact name on the box, so search works with brand names or ingredients, with examples to try. If the first match isn’t the right product, similar products are suggested, and a misspelled name returns “Did you mean…?” options instead of a dead end.

02

Understand the essentials

The page follows the order people would ask in: what is this medication, which side effects are common, and what’s serious? Common side effects appear in plain language, and each serious warning has a short “In short” summary. Boxed warnings are tagged in red, and longer details stay closed until opened.

03

Trust and transparency

In health, trust matters as much as clarity. Each result shows the label date and manufacturer and links to the full label, so the original text is one click away. A clear reminder says MedCheck is educational and not a substitute for medical advice.

THE PROCESS
RESEARCH INSIGHTS

Learning from how medication information is shown today

To understand how medication information is shown today, I reviewed leading health sites and the label data itself, looking for what helps people find answers fast.

KEY INSIGHT #1 The label has the answers but no structure. Every section is a long block of text, worded differently each time.
KEY INSIGHT #2 Strong sites rank information by importance. Drugs.com leads with boxed warnings and Mayo Clinic sorts by urgency.
KEY INSIGHT #3 Long detail works best on demand. WebMD tucks it into tabs, which keeps the first view short.
RESULT

Switching focus from showing the label to answering a question

The research changed how I framed the problem. People don’t want a better-looking label, they want an answer to “what should I watch for?” That shifted the design from reformatting the label to structuring it around that question: common side effects first, then serious warnings, with detail on demand.

It also changed what I left out. I tested openFDA’s adverse event reports, but those are submitted reports, not how often a side effect really happens, and they include entries like “drug ineffective.” Showing them could mislead, so they never made it into the design.

IDEATION

Finding the right amount of information

I explored three directions for the results page, judging each by one question: does it help someone find what to watch for quickly, without misleading them?

REACTIONS FIRST A top-10 chart of reported reactions and a symptom checker gave people everything at once. Too much to take in, and the chart implied causes it couldn’t prove.
BOXED WARNING FIRST Following Drugs.com, the boxed warning would lead the page. But people want to know what’s common first, so I kept common side effects on top and tagged boxed warnings in red.
ORDERED BY QUESTION Common side effects first, then serious warnings with boxed warnings tagged, then longer details, with the source last.

I chose the third direction because it matched how people think when they start a medication, and made it the basis for my wireframes.

USER FLOW

Designing for the moments that could go wrong

Mapping the experience end to end helped me design for the moments where it could go wrong. A person types a medication name, the product is found, and its label and drug class are retrieved. If nothing is found, they’re offered spelling suggestions to pick from, so they’re never left at a dead end.

THE DESIGN
WIREFRAMES

Starting with two key screens

I sketched two key screens in Figma: a home page and a medication page. The home page is a single search bar over a dark photo, inspired by Vrbo’s focused search. The medication page follows the order people would ask in, ending with the source.

VISUAL DESIGN

Creating a calm and trustworthy reading experience

MedCheck’s style guide pairs a clean white background with amber, red and blue accents to create a more approachable experience for the user. Amber marks common side effects, red marks serious warnings, and blue is used for links and buttons, with navy anchoring the home page and footer. Using very few cards groups the information, helps the user digest it effectively, and lets the content lead.

KEY TAKEAWAYS

🧩 Designing for every case, not just one

Every label is written differently, so a fix that worked for one medication could break another. I learned to design for the full range of real data, and built a tester that checks 78 medications so I could trust the design beyond a single example.

🔒 Clarity without changing the meaning

A summary that sounds right but changes the meaning is worse than a long one. In health design, clarity can’t come at the cost of accuracy, so every plain-language line traces back to the label, with the full text one click away.
